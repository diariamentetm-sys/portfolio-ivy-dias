After crying a little in the [last post](/en/entre-jornadas/por-que-a-area-de-produtos-nao-gosta-de-cx), I think I now have a duty to imagine what an ideal world between Product and CX would look like, right? (Rhetorical question, lol.)

I think, first of all, we should abolish the CX department. Nervous laughter. Let me explain.

CX should be a team person, a squad person, who takes an active part in the product, who breathes the agendas and evolutions of the journey they care for. They cannot sit on a distant lookout, wrapped in the customer’s cape, pointing at improvements with a selfie stick once a month. No way.

Like that parent who treats their child’s head with Escabin, CX must be embedded in the day-to-day of the journey where a given product, or set of products, lives. They need to go over it with a fine-tooth comb every day to find problems and seek solutions inside a map they carefully drew themselves.

When I say abolish the CX department, I’m not suggesting we end the discipline or its technical leadership. I’m talking about a structure where professionals live inside products, not in a separate department from which they watch journeys from afar.

I came up with a metaphor here. Maybe it will help me make myself clear.

## CX can be a plumber

Their goal this time is to keep the kitchen sink pipes flowing. But to do that, they need the complete map of the kitchen plumbing. Where does the water come from? Where does it go? What causes clogs? How do you fix each problem? What future improvements could keep those problems from repeating?

Inside the *house* product, that would be the kitchen blueprint, okay?

The plumber won’t make the homeowner (aka PM) memorize the kitchen blueprint. But they will explain what happened and what may have caused a given pipe failure.

That’s enough for the homeowner (aka PM) and the person responsible for the kitchen (aka PO) to understand what should be prioritized in that room’s improvement pipeline, which also includes remodeling the cabinets, replacing the tiles, and buying that gorgeous induction stove that will speed up the house’s culinary productions. (I’m going far, I know.)

There’s a catch, though: the people living in the house keep complaining that the sink water takes forever to drain. Meanwhile, the PO is focused on replacing the cabinets. The plumber, who knows the pipes and follows the problems in that space, can show that the remodel may end up compromised if the plumbing isn’t fixed first.

So see: the plumber gave enough grounding for the PM and the PO to discuss what should or shouldn’t be done. And in this case, they brought into the conversation a problem that directly affects the experience of whoever uses the kitchen.

If the PO has complementary notes, since they’re the one driving improvements in that space, they talk with the plumber and contribute hypotheses that can enrich the journey map.

The PM listens actively, weighs priorities on the house roadmap, and asks the plumber to share the plumbing journey — a lighter version of the blueprint — so they can consult it whenever they need to weigh new improvements.

The plumber also joins the discussions about solutions. After all, it wouldn’t make much sense to know every bend in the pipe, spot the problems, and disappear right when it’s time to decide how to fix them.

## A house that starts working better

The plumber was competent. They cleared the clog, mapped the kitchen plumbing blueprint and, for good measure, delivered a simplified plumbing journey to the PM.

The house PM and the kitchen PO agreed they were very effective. They mapped scenarios whose workings the others hadn’t even imagined, and now, with that knowledge, it became easier to decide what to prioritize in the room.

Before the new cabinets comes the kitchen plumbing, okay? It’s that, or we leave the kitchen with stainless-steel Itatiaia cabinets to avoid any catastrophe if the kitchen floods. Hehe.

From that experience with the plumber, the PM adopts the same process for the house bathrooms and the laundry area.

They bring in an electrician and a gas technician, recommended by the plumber, who work with the same methodology. Each specialist starts mapping the systems and journeys of the spaces under their care, following a shared documentation structure.

Little by little, the blueprints of each room begin to connect. The plumber knows the kitchen pipes, the bathroom specialist knows that room’s particularities, and the other professionals complement the map with their knowledge of the electrical network, piped gas, and other house systems.

From integrating those maps, the complete house blueprint is born. No specialist needs to master every detail alone, but everyone needs to understand how their part connects to the others.

**Each specialist knows their room deeply. Together, they know the house.**

![Integrated product and experience flow](/images/blog/fluxo-integrado-produtos-experiencias.jpg)

But during that work, the plumber notices something interesting: even after the repairs, water pressure in the kitchen keeps oscillating. Checking the integrated house blueprint, they see that the kitchen plumbing shares part of the hydraulic network with the bathroom.

They seek out the specialist responsible for that space and, together, investigate the problem. They discover the failure’s origin is in a shared stretch of pipe, outside both of their scopes.

The problem no longer belongs exclusively to the kitchen or the bathroom. Each room’s PO has their own priorities, and neither can solve the issue alone.

That’s when integrated mapping starts to make even more sense. The specialists share their knowledge, identify dependencies between journeys, and offer a view none of them would have working alone.

The PM now has material to discuss priorities with the people responsible for different rooms, weighing how each decision affects the house’s functioning.

## And who runs this house?

Plumbers, electricians, and gas technicians are the CX and UX specialists of the *house* product. And here’s a clarification: CX and UX are different disciplines, with their own competencies and responsibilities, but in this metaphor both can take the plumber’s role.

While CX watches the experience of whoever lives in the house — considering the relationship between rooms, services, and problems that cut across different journeys — UX goes deeper into how people interact with each of those spaces, investigating use difficulties and designing solutions for them.

That doesn’t mean one looks after the house and the other the faucet. Both can investigate the same problem, join the same discussions, and collaborate on building solutions, each contributing their technical repertoire.

But neither of them is a PM or a PO.

They are specialists who know the paths, spot risks, investigate problems, and take part in building solutions. They don’t need to own the roadmap to be responsible for experience quality.

Prioritization is driven by the POs, inside their respective products, aligned with the PM, who holds a holistic view of the house. That doesn’t mean decisions are made without the specialists. Quite the opposite. The closer they are to the discussions, the greater the chance priorities will consider real problems rather than assumptions about what needs improving.

I also don’t think CXs and UXs should be concentrated in a department with a leadership that alone holds the complete house blueprint.

I imagine a transversal leadership, responsible for technical governance, professional development, and consistency of methods. But the specialists need to live in the products, know their problems, and take part in everyday decisions.

The kitchen plumber shouldn’t discover there’s a bathroom problem only at the monthly plumbers’ department meeting. Specialists need to talk to each other, share their maps, and understand how an intervention in one room can affect the others.

Transversal leadership doesn’t need to own the complete house blueprint. Its role is to ensure specialists can build, integrate, and keep their maps updated, preserving each one’s technical autonomy and a shared view of the experience.

That leadership would also matter when problems cross a product’s boundaries. If a hydraulic network failure compromises three rooms, but no PO can prioritize the fix alone, someone needs to help take that discussion to a decision forum that considers the whole house.

It’s not about giving CX leadership the final say on product backlogs. It’s about making sure cross-cutting problems have visibility, defined owners, and follow-up, even when the solution depends on different teams.

And I see no need for a leadership that intermediates every conversation between the plumber and the kitchen PO. Craft autonomy should rest on the technical maturity and sense of responsibility inherent to these roles.

Governance exists to ensure the maps talk to each other, methods stay consistent, and knowledge produced by one specialist can be used by the others.

## After all, who do these professionals serve?

The house as a whole. PMs, POs, developers, and above all whoever lives in those spaces.

Because, at the end of the day, the resident doesn’t much care whether the plumber reports to the house PM, the kitchen PO, or an entire department of plumbers.

They just want to open the faucet and see water come out. Preferably without opening a ticket with three different departments.
