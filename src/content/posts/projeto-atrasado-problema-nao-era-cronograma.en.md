> **Editor’s note:** the names of the organization, teams, and professionals have been omitted. Some contextual details were generalized to protect confidentiality, without changing what the project taught us.

## The project that started with an idea and almost no questions

A large Brazilian company approached the consultancy where I worked to develop an artificial intelligence solution for risk assessment. There was a rough RFP and a few loosely structured ideas, but almost nothing formal or technical about the problem, the users, or how the solution was supposed to work.

It was that phase where you have a promising solution, an innovation target on the horizon, and a dangerously small number of answered questions. In my head, the request could have come from a Pinky and the Brain summit: “Call that consultancy. They’re probably starving for any gig with us.”

On the consultancy side, my manager staffed what was supposed to be the CX *dream team*: me as CX Lead, a senior UX Researcher, a UX Lead, and a PMO for damage control. On the client side, the Tech Lead, two Product Owners, and the project sponsor were involved.

In the first conversation, I presented a Design Thinking approach. The reaction came fast:

> “I’m not sure that’s necessary. We already have the problem identified, and for me we’d go straight to the prototype.”

I explained that stepping back a few squares didn’t mean ignoring the scope we’d received. It meant checking whether the proposed solution answered the right problem and whether every affected area was represented. The sponsor backed the approach and validated starting with investigation.

Leadership on their side bankrolled the process. The ball was back with us. We went off to build the timeline, still innocent and happy.

## A simple timeline for a context that wasn’t simple

The initial proposal covered roughly two months from immersion to a navigable prototype. Honest rice-and-beans work:

- focus groups with five stakeholder groups;
- journey and persona mapping;
- Lean Inception to define the MVP;
- a co-creation session focused on the interface;
- high-fidelity prototyping;
- agile qualitative validation.

From the start, we made it explicit that the project would need continuous collaboration from Product Owners, product management, technical leadership, and data science. You can’t design an AI solution by isolating experience, business, and technical feasibility.

Even so, pressure appeared to put the development team to work immediately:

> “We need to shorten the timeline as much as possible. Can’t we put the developers to work with you while you do Design Thinking?”

I proposed active participation from the data scientist during Lean Inception, plus earlier involvement through interview extracts, journeys, and personas. I also explained that I couldn’t confirm development availability because I represented the CX track.

Then I got an internal call: “You absolutely cannot say that. The client can’t know we’re different teams.” The guidance came from account management, which moved smoothly between timeline policing and tourist appearances on the project. Showing up for the sponsor? Not so much.

The episode looked small, but it already delivered part of the plot: we had to sell an integrated delivery that wasn’t fully integrated even inside our own house. It was the first “it’ll be fine” said with a raised eyebrow.

I went back to the plan and proposed something close to *dual track*: CX would lead discovery and, once we had the first MVP prototype and a roadmap, development would come in to structure the solution architecture. From that point, part of the CX team would stay on continuous improvement and another part would follow the build.

The timeline stretched from two months to four, with development scheduled to join from the second month. The plan was approved. What nobody predicted was that agenda delays, missing information, and reschedules would make discovery alone consume nearly those four months.

Great. Timeline approved. Little did we know the timeline was the most optimistic character in that story.

## The first month of a project that hadn’t started yet

After approval, we reinforced the agreement: from then on, client and consultancy should operate as one team.

Almost a month passed with no materials, no additional context, and no information that would help the team move. From the PO, sepulchral silence. Then, suddenly, the first focus group appeared on the calendar. One area had been summoned to talk to us while we still had little beyond the original RFP. Pure chaos. It was what we had for the day.

In the first session, I introduced the team and explained the dynamic. The PO and the sponsor didn’t contextualize the project or our presence beforehand. Luckily, the first group was made of experienced professionals who were available and generous with their answers.

Without enough information and without the PO mediation we expected, we focused questions on participants’ day-to-day: activities, responsibilities, needs, pains, and decisions. We avoided nudging them to confirm an AI solution already imagined by someone else.

Some questions still weren’t calibrated well enough. At times, participants didn’t know whether they were answering what we expected. We had to hold the uncertainty and explain more than once:

> “There’s no right or wrong answer here. We need to understand your day-to-day so we can map journeys, needs, and difficulties.”

Despite the conditions, the first immersion produced relevant material. On arid ground, without PO support, but surrounded by polite people willing to collaborate, we got four journeys and the first personas. Pollyanna? Maybe. But it worked.

Craft wasn’t the biggest obstacle. The problem was working without the context and preparation that should have come before the conversation.

## How do you interview stakeholders without knowing who the stakeholders are?

After the first area, we tried to move to the second. That’s when we learned the team was too busy and might not be available anytime soon.

The information opened basic questions that still had no answers: what was that area’s role? Was it essential to the project? Who had decision power? Who would be affected by the solution? What was the relationship between the different structures involved?

The UX Researcher suggested we build a stakeholder map with the PO. We asked him to help us understand who was in the project and to what extent each area was a protagonist, influencer, or user of the future solution.

The answer deserves a frame:

> “I thought you were going to bring me that information.”

We scratched our ears to check we’d heard right. The expectation was that we’d discover, during the focus groups themselves, who those people were and what role they played. But how do you ask the right questions if you don’t even know who you’re talking to?

The company’s Tech Lead had a moment of clarity and explained that this information had to come from the PO. We later received a rather thin org chart. Names and hierarchy were there. What each area did in the project? Nothing.

We looked up public sources to understand some terms and structures in that sector. We found part of the answers online, which was useful and also a little embarrassing. Don’t try this at home. This was a Mayday case, not a recommended method.

The first lesson became clear: **research doesn’t start in the interview. It starts in field preparation.**

## When the calendar reveals the project’s real priority

Another area couldn’t join because its main leader was on vacation. The guidance was to leave it for the end. The sponsor asked whether that would affect the timeline.

I said yes. We already had two unavailable areas. I suggested bringing forward the last accessible one and handling the two missing areas in a second wave. The proposal was refused because they didn’t want to split the project into stages.

So we waited. Because, apparently, the calendar also had decision power on the project.

Meanwhile, we refined journeys and personas from the first track, produced meeting notes, tracked hours, and prepared the weekly status. Part of the operational management that should have sat with the PMO also landed on my lap. At the time I took it as friendship. Today I recognize friendship shouldn’t count as an official project-management tool.

We finally got to talk to another area. Before the session, we were warned that the leader was direct, demanding, and had little patience for long conversations. The expectation was a difficult meeting. The result was the opposite: he and the team collaborated a lot. Two conversations produced five personas, five micro-journeys, and five cross journeys.

That contrast also taught something important: **a stakeholder’s reputation doesn’t replace direct experience with them**. A leader labeled “difficult” can collaborate when they understand the purpose and find a well-facilitated conversation.

The other areas were released almost a month later. One of them booked and canceled so often it was turning into legend. Everyone talked about the tower. Getting a conversation with it? No one could.

When we finally interviewed the team whose leader had been on vacation, we hit the strongest resistance of the immersion. The stakeholder kept asking:

> “Why do I need to answer this?”  
> “Where are these questions going?”  
> “I don’t understand the usefulness of this conversation.”

We explained the process, the mapping goal, and why we still weren’t jumping ahead to a closed solution. Even with the resistance, we collected enough information to move forward.

By the end of the immersion, we had 17 personas, 17 micro-journeys, and 22 cross journeys.

The volume of material could suggest a successful stage. And in terms of production, it was. But the wear didn’t come from the work’s complexity. It came from the lack of mediation between the project team and the participating areas.

There was no consistent *pre-game*. Interviewees weren’t properly prepared to understand why they were there, what would be done with their input, and how much involvement the next stages would require.

Behind the scenes, word circulated that some areas had innovation targets tied to internal incentives, while others had already met those targets or feared the initiative would interfere with their own indicators. We never had enough evidence to confirm every detail. Still, the observed behavior showed that the areas didn’t necessarily share the same interest in the project’s success.

The second lesson was less comfortable: **being included in the same project doesn’t mean sharing the same goal.** Sometimes it doesn’t even mean sharing the will to be in the project.

## The development team that could only exist after the MVP

While CX moved forward, another problem approached: where was the development team promised in the timeline?

The data scientist and the Tech Lead were direct: without a defined MVP, it wouldn’t be possible to determine the profiles needed for the build.

That statement contradicted the approved plan. We already knew we’d work with artificial intelligence and that the front end would use React Native. Still, development wouldn’t join at that moment.

The issue wasn’t only technical. Engineering’s absence during discovery increased the risk of designing a desirable solution that sat far from the real constraints, dependencies, and possibilities of the architecture.

There was also a very concrete impact on progress: the interface and handoff documentation were ready, but the front-end team couldn’t start. We had the map, the screens, and the travel instructions. What was missing was releasing whoever was supposed to put the car on the road.

We had created a timeline that promised parallel collaboration. In practice, we were back to working in sequence.

The third lesson showed up there: **dual track doesn’t start existing because someone drew two parallel lines on a pretty timeline. It depends on availability, ownership, and real participation.** Without that, it’s just corporate decoration.

## Lean Inception and the moment collaboration left the room

We moved on to Lean Inception. The script had been approved by the PO, who at that moment seemed closer and more collaborative.

The joy didn’t last. More precisely, it lasted until the first call.

In the first session, the leader of one area returned to questioning their presence:

> “I don’t know what I’m doing here.”

I explained again the goal, why that area mattered, and how decisions would be built. We got their consent to continue. Then another leader, who until then had collaborated, refused to join the activities:

> “I can’t waste time on this. When everything is defined, call me to approve.”

They left the meeting and took at least four team members with them. A real herd effect — only inside a calendar invite.

I asked the PO for support to reinforce the need for participation. The intervention was cautious and not enough to reverse the decision. This time, prior explanation hadn’t been missing: we had communicated the purpose, described the activities, and shown that this was exactly the space to shape the solution together.

What was missing wasn’t information. What was missing was an organizational agreement strong enough to sustain collaboration when it stopped being convenient.

After the session, we held a learnings conversation with the PO and the sponsor. We reinforced that the timeline and approach had been approved from the start. Without participation from the areas, we’d face rework, gaps, and possible blockers during the build.

It was so basic to have to explain that I almost felt silly saying it. The timeline had been approved, the process was collaborative, and people needed to collaborate. Still, there we were, defending again the minimum conditions of an agreement everyone had accepted in PowerPoint.

## The right way and the possible way

We went back to the drawing board. We used the available information to fill part of the Lean Inception canvases and then validated the hypotheses with the areas that had left the meetings.

Was it the right way? Not exactly.

It was the way that worked. We filled the canvases with the resources we had and then chased validations afterward. It worked, but it charged interest.

There’s a fairly common resistance to co-creation dynamics. Some people look at sticky notes, music, and collaborative activities as if someone had brought a field day into the board meeting. I even heard: “Do we really need music while we write?” What kind of person doesn’t like music? But fine — that wasn’t the project’s main risk.

Other people prefer to receive a finished solution and occupy only the comfortable approval seat. Taking part in the build is work. Approving at the end lets you disagree with everything with the elegance of someone who just arrived.

But withdrawing from the build doesn’t remove those people’s influence. It only moves their questions to a more expensive moment in the project.

The fourth lesson was practical: **when co-creation doesn’t happen at the intended moment, the decision doesn’t disappear; it comes back later as review, objection, or rework.**

## A two-year product presented as a set of screens

After that turbulence, we entered a finer alignment phase. We crossed experience, technology, and business, sequenced features, and consolidated a backlog with enough material for at least two years of evolution.

We defined a main MVP as the backbone and identified 15 future epics for the solution’s evolution. The UX Lead turned the work into a consistent prototype, and validation was positive, though there were still questions about the process.

We also delivered opportunity trees to guide future discussions, believing that content would be used before the Second Coming. More than an interface, we were leaving a product vision, documented decisions, and paths for continuity.

In the final weeks, we again recommended keeping experience professionals present during development.

The answer was direct:

> “We don’t need that. We already have the essential screens. From here we can move on without you.”

It was a mix of relief at no longer negotiating every step with the opposing PO, and regret at seeing very good work reduced to “pretty screens.” Months of investigation, alignment, journeys, hypotheses, decisions, and prioritization disappeared in one sentence. In the end, that’s hardly news in the design world.

It’s a known distortion in design: the organization takes part in a strategic process, but at implementation time it keeps only the visual manifestation.

Two months later, I was already moving to Portugal and had left the consultancy. That’s when the company realized, rather unhappily, that the project wouldn’t continue on delivered screens alone and came back asking for the team’s support.

The product had an interface. What it still didn’t have was experience continuity.

Those who lived it, lived it.

## The problem was never just the timeline

The timeline slipped, but not because the Design Thinking process had too many stages. It slipped because the conditions required to run those stages were never truly secured.

The project suffered from:

- decisions approved but not sustained;
- stakeholders invited without preparation;
- unclear responsibilities;
- areas with different interests and incentives;
- development promised but absent from discovery;
- co-creation treated as optional;
- experience reduced to interface delivery.

The PO didn’t need to master every answer. They needed to build the field so answers could be found. The sponsor didn’t need to facilitate the sessions. They needed to protect the participation agreement. Stakeholders didn’t need to love the method. They needed to understand which decisions depended on them and what the consequences of their absence would be.

## What I would do differently today

If I ran a similar project again, I’d include a few conditions before even closing the timeline:

### 1. An explicit discovery agreement

A short document with goal, stages, participants, responsibilities, required availability, and decisions expected from each group.

### 2. Stakeholder mapping before interviews

Not only names and titles, but influence, interest, knowledge, decision power, and expected impact of the solution.

### 3. Mandatory preparation of participating areas

A short conversation before sessions to explain context, expectations, dynamics, how information will be used, and participants’ roles.

### 4. Clear decision criteria

Define who recommends, who contributes, who decides, and who only needs to be informed. Diffuse approval usually produces continuous contestation.

### 5. Organizational risks inside project status

Stakeholder absence, missing materials, low availability, and conflicts of interest should appear formally as risks, with timeline impact.

### 6. Technical participation from investigation onward

Even without a full squad, engineering and data science need to follow hypotheses, constraints, and decisions before the MVP is consolidated.

### 7. A continuity plan before handoff

The prototype can’t be treated as the end of experience work. You need to define how decisions will be followed, tested, and adjusted during development.

## The framework doesn’t protect the project alone

The project delivered personas, journeys, opportunities, backlog, MVP, and prototype. From an artifact perspective, there was outcome. But the experience taught something no canvas solves alone:

> A collaborative process only works when the organization also takes responsibility for collaborating.

Design Thinking, Lean Inception, dual track, and research don’t fail only because of methodological flaws. They also fail when they’re approved as discourse but not sustained as practice.

The problem wasn’t the timeline. The timeline only made visible everything the organization still hadn’t agreed on.
