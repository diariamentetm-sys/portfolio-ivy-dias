“Nobody loves me, nobody wants me” could be the theme song of CX inside companies. From the experiences I’ve had, some more than others, the Customer Experience professional often lands as persona non grata. To exercise a bit of empathy: no area likes having its flaws pointed out by another that claims to hold the framework of the customer’s voice. But someone has to do that dirty work, right?

Working mostly in digital products, I have my own observations. Inside squads, for example, people don’t like a continuous-improvement backlog being generated by someone from outside, someone who isn’t in the engine room. At a large bank where I looked after three products at once as a UX 360 (the kind who researches and designs screens at the same time), Product Owners often asked us to run NPS research, usability tests, and quantitative studies. The intention was for the backlog to be steered by the POs themselves, not by the PMs, who officially owned bringing CX insights in.

> **Note:** I once worked with a dear PO who vented to me that, in his view, he wasn’t an errand boy. He saw himself as the product’s PM, because he considered “PO” a more “junior” way of titling professionals who lead products.

At another financial institution there was a real clash, Game of Thrones style. “CX doesn’t touch anything here,” barked a PM who managed the product area as a whole. Little did he know the CX team had already reached out to me and included me in the company’s biweekly closed-loop discussions. In that role, as a strategic UX, they saw me as a facilitating channel to bring product information in and take customer-voice questions back to the area. In other words, without meaning to, I became a double agent. What ate at the PM had a name: jealousy over not holding the truth he believed he owned. Needless to say, the place was going through a digital transformation whose working model was “cascágil” — waterfall wearing agile clothes.

In another experience, already coordinating CX inside the company (and mind you, my position was never comfortable, whether inside CX or as UX on a product), I became an enemy of the state for some leaders, because customer reports were blunt about what actually needed to be done.

Add my particular way of delivering information, and I imagine it didn’t sound great to hear: “The products offered for redemption here are like leftover party favors from a June festival.” I didn’t speak that way out of sadism, but because I saw it as a more playful way to surface problems and lighten the blow of a raw truth: “Your points-redemption product shelf is painfully bad.”

In that same situation, my manager — whose reasoning I still find ignoble and still don’t understand — put me in charge of a closed-loop agenda with a group of coordinators who had no final decision power over the actions required.

No methodology could save it. The move was low, because that decision shielded managers from the improvement actions that needed urgent attention. The clever trick was to camouflage what would reach the board, which had weekly accountability agendas. With one more barrier in place, information was filtered and chosen by eeny-meeny-miny-moe. While what really needed to change didn’t change, NPS (ah, someone remembered NPS?) got worse every month, as expected.

In the other piece I wrote here, [“How I showed what CX was in a place where nobody knew if it was something to eat or put in your hair”](/en/entre-jornadas/como-mostrei-o-que-era-cx), I told how a Squad Lead tore into me because I had found a flaw in a flow that left the product she owned and crossed into another product’s gate.

That was small potatoes next to the larger context I was in. When I shared customer-voice insights with the POs of those areas, they didn’t take the change suggestions to the marketing and product teams requesting those pages. Not out of incompetence. They were coached to filter problems so those areas would interfere less with the site.

Well then: nothing changes but the company registry number.

After so many similar situations, I started asking myself whether the problem is really CX’s presence, or the discomfort of having someone question decisions already made. Listening to the customer sounds beautiful in institutional speeches; it’s harder when the customer starts talking about exactly what nobody wants to touch.

And maybe that’s the curiousest part of this relationship. Product and CX should care about the same thing, but in some companies they seem to fight over who has more authority to say what the customer needs.

In the end, research, dashboards, NPS, closed loop, and a beautiful results deck are useless if the customer’s voice can only travel as far as internal politics allow.

Don’t shoot the messenger, we come in peace
