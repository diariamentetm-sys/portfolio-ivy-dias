## Arriving as CX without finding a defined territory

In November 2022 I started working as CX on the website of a telecom company. I got through the interview after a half-hour conversation with the area manager, who explained what she expected from the role and also told me it would be a dual-hat job: besides owning CX for the company’s portal, I would also work in the company’s CoE. I remember that, in the interview, she also said: “There’s another opportunity in here that I think you might fit as well.” I answered: “Look, I really enjoyed talking with you. I’d rather work under your management, because I feel we already had a lot of chemistry in this short conversation.”

So I started “working” on a nice Thursday in the week of All Souls’ Day. I partially had my accesses, even though I wasn’t on the team Slack yet. Through my personal Teams, I reached out to the manager: “Hey, I’m available — if you want to include me in any meeting, I’m around.” I didn’t get an immediate reply. Actually, I think I still haven’t gotten a reply in that chat to this day heheh.

I thought: what can I do to start understanding where I am? I know — I’ll map the product journey, or build a sitemap of the portal, something that gives me a sense of the ground ahead. I opened Miro and started screenshotting screen by screen, checking flows coming out of the main sections and reflecting on what counted as top of funnel, what kind of discourse showed up in the copy, the visual communication of the page, the banners, the sales cards. The site was extremely complex, to say the least. Even though I’d been a customer of the company for more than 9 years, there was so much there I’d never heard of that the chill down my spine was real — I won’t deny it. “Where did I tie my donkey?” I kept mapping the portal journey anyway, even with fear.

## Mapping the problem was the first way to show my role

The following week, the portal tech lead came to talk to me. He gave me a really good task — one I could barely imagine would turn into an endeavor that wouldn’t be solved within my 2 years in the role. “We have a messy issue between the portal and the logged-in customer areas. Can you help us identify and document these problems?” “Sure, let’s go,” I answered, hungry like an astronaut getting her first mission to Mars.

Back in Miro, I mapped the user journey from the site into the logged-in areas. I dug through the flows top to bottom, identifying errors and severities between the logged-out and logged-in portals. When I showed it to the tech lead, he was really happy with what he saw and showed it to the portal Squad Lead, who until then hadn’t seen my work — she’d only been introduced to me by the manager. She also liked it a lot and said: “That’s exactly what we needed.”

A meeting was called with the involved areas — residential and mobile products on the logged-out side, plus the POs of the logged-in areas. I was introduced in the meeting and had my moment to show the mapped points, even though a lot was still being absorbed by me as product context.

Well, I can be really useful here, I thought.

The feeling of finally taking off and claiming even a small space inside a new team is a lot like discovering a new band and loving it right away. Feels like home.

## From flow mapping to decisions about the portal experience

A few days later, the portal CMS PO introduced herself and showed how the component intake flow worked inside the site. She said she felt the need for someone who could facilitate discussions about new components coming in, because we needed to anticipate scalability as much as possible for new builds and changes to existing components — since the work would be done four-handed with the company’s Design System team, which was already highly demanded. There, another role opened where I would matter a lot to the team. I was thrilled.

I met the agency traffic manager who handled layouts for the portal pages. Even with a content team for each portal section, there was still an agency adapting campaigns into site banners and laying out pages. The content editors approved or rejected layouts and, once everything was ok — meaning approved by the requesting area and also by the Squad Lead — they published the pages in the CMS.

The traffic manager told me the agency had a hard time shipping simple, easy-to-read pages. The medicine-leaflet look and massive blocks of text were, most of the time, a house requirement — whether from the product area requesting the page, or from how marketing, SEO, or the content editor for that portal area interpreted the brief. That’s when I felt the hole went deeper, and that what was obvious from a UX lens might not even be saved by Nielsen’s heuristics.

## Using research to put the customer’s voice into the routine

In my second month, the manager announced we would bring CX metrics back into the monthly reports to the area’s leadership. I had already analyzed the on-site questionnaires measuring CSAT and flagged, in a document, attention points that needed fixing so we could get clean answers about what belonged to the portal’s scope.

Then it was time to put the metrics back on the site, now reconfigured with a more accurate approach. The approach was fairly shy: three questionnaires across Customer Service, Residential, and TV & Content. It was a way to step into the still-cold water of the content area, since I was already quite active with the CMS team’s demands.

I thought they wouldn’t take me seriously after the agency traffic manager’s warning. But, step by step, I built a routine: I ran the research through the month and, within the first fifteen days, there were already some signals about the pages.

And, to my surprise, if there had been toilet paper with the company name on it, the questionnaires would have captured customer comments about it. In other words, the portal was a huge catalyst for problems across the company as a whole.

With that volume of information, I set another routine: a two-hour call to present research insights to the content areas that owned those pages. There I showed the main points about content, usability, product, and customer service. Most insights said more about the post-sales pipeline than about the top of the funnel, even though there were still big learnings there.

## Getting insights out of the report and into operations

The content editors saw value in the research and, little by little, the water that had been cold started warming up. With a few learnings under our belt, I felt it was the right moment to create a second recurring session with them, called Task Force, covering brainstorming for page improvements that could also serve as a lab for other structural page changes.

All of that excited me a lot. No call blocked the Squad Lead from joining, but her participation was optional, because my clients in that moment were the content editors. The idea was not to interfere with a channel that already existed between them and the Squad Lead; my role there was consulting on the pages.

Truth be told, I’ve always believed that big movements inside companies start from the bottom up — not by waiting for a yes from managers while the “engine room” hasn’t absorbed the movement into its own routine.

## When identifying the problem doesn’t mean being able to change it

But every month there was the report to leadership, and the goals were hit only timidly. Some months yes, others no. Major structural changes happened only in customer service, which gave us solid results measured in A/B tests by the CRM area.

What was clear to me should be done — because it had worked in one area — generated resistance in the other areas where research ran, and changes happened drop by drop. At the same time, I couldn’t simply say in a leadership report that change was slow, because it also wouldn’t be fair to name the portal team as the main culprit for structural changes not happening. There was a giant mechanism behind it, in layers like product and marketing — those ones full of interference, lack of awareness, and influence, all very over and in equal measure.

## When CX topics started showing up in the team’s planning

Every three months the portal held quarterly planning. A very rewarding moment was noticing that the areas where research ran started bringing topics discussed in the Task Force agendas. I don’t know if it was at the Squad Lead’s request, but I loved seeing the discussions gain strength and surprise management.

It seemed that maybe she was getting a bit more contact with what I’d been working on, but until then she didn’t know I had that routine with the team.

## Turning scattered needs into a shared vision for the team

I finally built a CX opportunity tree from all the insights we’d discussed with the content editors, the need for new components, the flow reviews to make the path between portals more fluid, and the inclusion of flows that didn’t exist yet but were already aspirations for other people on the team.

The Scrum Master bought the idea easily and turned it into something bigger for the whole team to see and act on.

I believe the CX role isn’t to play Eureka for the team, pulling everything out of your own hat. It’s a role that acts as a spokesperson for collective needs — everyone involved in the process and, above all, the customers. So a lot of what was there wasn’t invented; it was a simple exercise of observation and fitting pieces together with the resources I had.

## The space CX won and what remained from that experience

I’ll wrap up here because the rest is routine. There were moments when, for lack of company culture, I was questioned about what I was doing there — like when, while mapping flows, I found a break between a CTA and the residential checkout funnel.

I pushed back: “What do you expect a CX to do?” — among other small northwestern climates hehe.

But, in the end, I left a team I missed as much as I believe I left my professional signature on. I was useful down to the last conducting wire of my work.

Being CX is a role with a lot of autonomy and a sense of leadership without really carrying the weight of management. That bothers some people, but most of the time I have more fans than haters in the process.

Those who lived it, lived my way.
